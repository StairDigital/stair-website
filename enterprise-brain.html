The Enterprise Brain

Every enterprise has data.
Few have a memory.

Thirty years of decisions sit in your systems, and almost none of it can be recalled when it matters. The Enterprise Brain is the layer that turns that scattered record into one governed memory, and a small language model of your own, trained on your work and running inside your estate, that lets your people and your agents ask it questions in plain language.

Unified memory Your own small model Cited answers Runs in your estate

Why this exists

The problem was never storage. It was recall.

Large organisations have spent two decades getting good at keeping things. They have not spent them getting good at finding things, and the gap shows up in the same three places in almost every enterprise we open up.

It is scattered

The answer to a single board question lives across the ERP, a shared drive, four years of minutes, a contract nobody has opened since signing and one person's inbox. No system holds all of it, so nobody can answer it quickly and honestly.

It cannot be trusted on sight

A number in a deck is only as good as the source behind it, and by the third slide nobody remembers where it came from. Anything a board acts on has to be able to show its working, which is exactly what most AI tools cannot do.

It walks out of the door

The person who knows why a decision was taken in 2019 retires, and the reasoning goes with them. Institutional knowledge is held in people rather than in the institution, and every exit is a quiet write-down.

What it is

Three layers. One accountable memory.

The Enterprise Brain is not another place to put documents. It sits above the systems you already run, reads from them continuously, and becomes the single thing that everything else asks.

  1. Ground it in what you already run

    Connectors read from the ERP, the document estate, the contract repository, board packs and mail. Nothing is migrated and nothing is replaced, because a memory that requires a migration never gets built.

  2. Give it structure, not just search

    A semantic index makes text findable by meaning. An entity graph on top of it knows that a vendor, a contract, a payment and a board resolution are the same story, so the brain can follow a thread rather than return a list of files.

  3. Inherit your permissions, keep the trail

    The brain answers each person only from what that person is already entitled to see, and every answer carries its sources. Nothing becomes visible because it was indexed.

The SLM capability

The biggest model is rarely the right one.

A small language model is one trained tightly on a narrow domain rather than on everything. For an enterprise that is not a compromise. All of its capacity goes to your work instead of to poetry and trivia, it runs on hardware you already own, and it is small enough to be frozen, versioned and audited like any other control. STAIR builds them.

01

Right-sized, not downsized

We measure the task before choosing the model. A frontier model sets the standard on your own questions, then we distil that behaviour down into something small enough to own. What you end up with is not a weaker model; it is one that spends everything it has on your work.

  • Task-level benchmarking
  • Distillation
  • Open-weight base
02

Runs where your data already lives

Small enough for a single GPU in your own data centre, a modest instance in your cloud tenancy, or a box on the plant floor. Regulated material never crosses the boundary your auditors already understand, and no per-token meter runs against a vendor you do not control.

  • On-premise and in-VPC
  • Edge deployment
  • No per-token meter
03

Fluent in your language, not everyone's

Trained on your contracts, your policies, your minutes and your house style, so it uses your vocabulary and your approval thresholds. Narrow beats broad here: a small model tuned on one enterprise's corpus often outperforms a far larger general model on that enterprise's own work.

  • Domain tuning
  • Retrieval over the brain
  • House style and tone
04

Frozen, versioned, auditable

A model you own is an artifact you can pin. Freeze a version for a year and the answer it gave in March can still be reproduced in December. That is the difference between a tool and a control, and it is what lets the second and third lines of defence sign off on it.

  • Pinned versions
  • Reproducible answers
  • Audit logging

Frontier models still have their place, and we use them: to set the benchmark, to distil from, and for genuinely open-ended work. What they should not be is the thing your close cycle depends on.

Your corpus trains your model, and the model belongs to you. It does not train anyone else's, and it does not leave with us. This is the first question every board asks, and it should be. The architecture on this page exists so the answer can be given plainly, in writing, and shown to hold in the deployment itself.

Why it can be used at board level

An answer you cannot check is not an answer.

Most enterprise AI fails its first real test in the same way: it is fluent, it is plausible, and it cannot tell you where it got that from. The brain is built the other way round, with the citation as the point rather than as a footnote.

  • Every claim carries its sourceAnswers are assembled from retrieved documents, and each statement links back to the page, clause or line it came from. If a source cannot be shown, the claim is not made.
  • It declines rather than guessesWhere the memory has nothing to support an answer, the brain says so. A confident wrong answer costs far more than a blank one in the settings we work in.
  • Access is inherited, not grantedThe brain never widens what someone can see. Two people asking the same question get answers drawn only from their own entitlements.
  • The trail survives the answerQuestion, sources, model version and the person who asked are all logged, so an internal auditor can reconstruct any answer months later. This is the same discipline set out in The AI Auditor.

How it lands

Narrow first. Then wide.

A brain built for the whole enterprise on day one is a programme that never ships. We start with one function where the memory is already painful, prove it against that function's own standard, and widen from something that works.

Phase one

Ground

Map where knowledge actually lives, connect the first sources, and stand the memory up against one function, most often finance, audit or legal. Permissions and provenance are built in at this point, not retrofitted later.

Ends with: a working memory over real sources, and the questions it must answer
Phase two

Prove

Run it against the questions that function is asked every month, side by side with how they are answered today. The evaluation harness is written here, so improvement becomes measurable rather than anecdotal.

Ends with: measured accuracy, a cost per answer, and a decision on what it may act on
Phase three

Widen

Extend the memory across functions and put agents on top of it, each one operating inside the same permissions and the same audit trail. The brain becomes the thing every new AI initiative connects to rather than another silo beside them.

Ends with: a shared memory the rest of the AI estate is built on
